Technical Policy Proposal: AI-Driven Location Tracking and Algorithmic Privacy
Statement of the Problem
In the modern digital economy, smartphones act as persistent tracking devices, continuously transmitting precise geolocation data to a hidden ecosystem of third-party data brokers. While location tracking is not a new phenomenon, the integration of artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning into this sector has created a severe ethical crisis. The problem lies in how commercial data brokers use AI algorithms to aggregate, de-anonymize, and analyze billions of raw location pings to infer deeply intimate details about users' lives. By identifying patterns in spatial data, these AI systems can predictably reveal users' visits to reproductive health clinics, places of worship, domestic violence shelters, and addiction treatment centers.
The scope of this issue primarily encompasses the commercial ad-tech industry, mobile application developers, and massive data broker firms such as Kochava and Outlogic. Unlike state-sponsored surveillance, which is bound by constitutional constraints such as the Fourth Amendment, this corporate tracking operates in a largely unregulated gray area. Consumers technically "consent" to location sharing through lengthy and opaque terms of service, but they do not consent to having their movements continuously analyzed by AI to create and sell sensitive behavioral profiles.
Addressing this ethical challenge is critical because the unregulated sale of AI-processed location data poses immediate threats to human rights, physical safety, and civil liberties. It allows targeted advertisers, malicious actors, or political vigilantes to purchase access to the most private aspects of an individual's life. Focusing on commercial data brokers highlights a dangerous regulatory loophole where AI capabilities have vastly outpaced existing privacy laws, making it a critical boundary for urgent policy intervention.

Examples of the Problem
To analyze how commercial data brokers leverage AI to exploit mobile tracking data, two recent Federal Trade Commission (FTC) enforcement actions serve as foundational case studies.
The first event is FTC v. Kochava, Inc., a landmark regulatory lawsuit filed in August 2022 and settled in June 2026. The FTC charged the Idaho-based data broker with unfairly selling raw, timestamped GPS coordinates tied to unique Mobile Advertising IDs (MAIDs). This data allowed buyers to effortlessly trace individuals to highly sensitive locations, including reproductive health clinics, places of worship, and domestic violence shelters. Because this litigation spanned four years and concluded with a historic settlement, research resources are vast. There are over 30 major news publications (e.g., The New York Times, Wired, Reuters), numerous official FTC court filings, and at least six peer-reviewed legal and ethical journal articles analyzing how Kochava redefines consumer "harm" in the digital age.
The second event is the FTC’s January 2024 settlement with Outlogic, LLC (formerly X-Mode Social). In its first-ever ban on sensitive location data sales, the FTC alleged that Outlogic used Software Development Kits (SDKs) embedded in ordinary mobile apps to silently harvest precise coordinates. The firm then used machine learning to categorize users into sensitive behavioral "audience segments" (e.g., medical clinic visitors) and sold this data downstream to government contractors and advertisers. Because of its precedent-setting nature, this event is highly documented. There are approximately 20 national news reports, extensive documentation from legal defense groups like the ACLU, and four scholarly computer science papers focusing on SDK data-harvesting mechanics and algorithmic profile creation. Both events provide a robust empirical foundation to explore the systemic ethical vulnerabilities of modern commercial tracking.

Policy as Solution
While technological self-regulation and individual privacy tools exist, public policy represents the only viable, systemic solution to the ethical threats posed by AI-driven location tracking. In a market where personal location data is highly commodified, data brokers and ad-tech firms face intense economic pressure to maximize data collection. Voluntary industry guidelines or "self-regulation" are fundamentally insufficient because they lack enforcement mechanisms and fail to counteract these profit incentives. Furthermore, the traditional privacy paradigm of "notice and consent"—where users accept complex, fifty-page terms of service—is a systemic failure. Consumers cannot reasonably foresee that granting a weather app access to their GPS coordinates will result in an AI broker de-anonymizing their data to track a medical visit. Because the asymmetry of power between multi-billion-dollar tech conglomerates and individual consumers is so vast, intervention must occur at the state or federal level.
Policy can effectively address this problem by shifting the burden of protection from the individual consumer to the data collector. First, comprehensive federal legislation must explicitly define precise geolocation data as "sensitive personal information," placing strict limits on its collection, transfer, and sale. Second, policy must target the proprietary artificial intelligence models that monetize this data. A crucial policy mechanism to introduce is algorithmic disgorgement. This regulatory action forces data brokers to not only delete illegally obtained location databases but also to completely destroy the machine learning models trained on that illicit data.
By mandating that AI cannot profit from ethically compromised training sets, policy strikes at the economic heart of the data broker business model. Ultimately, while software developers can create localized "privacy-focused" patches, only legally binding public policy has the authority to permanently restrict commercial location tracking, standardize algorithmic accountability, and safeguard fundamental civil liberties in the modern AI era.
