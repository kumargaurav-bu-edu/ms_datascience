Week 10 Overview
This week’s content focuses on the concept of social credit systems (SCSs) and their role in addressing societal challenges. Through case studies, readings, and interactive discussions, we’ll examine how these systems function, the societal problems they aim to solve, and their reliance on social surveillance. We’ll also analyze the potential benefits of these systems, as well as the challenges they pose, including concerns about privacy, fairness, and the risks of misuse. 

This week’s readings introduce key concepts that will deepen your understanding of Social Credit and Surveillance. As you explore the materials, take time to reflect on how these ideas connect to your work and discuss your insights with classmates on Yellowdig and in the live sessions.


Learning Objectives
At the end of this week, you should be able to:

Define social credit systems.
Identify the underlying problem that social credit systems try to solve.
Explain the connection between social credit systems and social surveillance.

Video| NBC News. (2019, June 5). A look inside China’s social credit system [Video]. YouTube.

This NBC News video provides an in-depth examination of China's social credit system, raising several critical ethical questions relevant to data science. The journalist explores the ethical implications of this system, particularly how it challenges traditional notions of data privacy and individual autonomy. Additionally, the video delves into the potential biases in the algorithms used and their impact on different groups, highlighting the fairness and equity of such a system. 

Read |Cho, E. (2024, December 11). The social credit system: Not just another Chinese idiosyncrasy. Journal of Public and International Affairs.  

This article discusses China's SCS and its broader implications. It explains how the SCS, a data-driven project designed to monitor, assess, and influence the behavior of citizens and enterprises, represents a new frontier in digital surveillance. The article raises critical questions about the role of data technology in society, not just in China but also in democratic countries like the United States. The author argues for the need for a comprehensive data protection framework in the U.S. to balance democracy, security, and growth. 

Read |Matsakis, L. (2019, July 25). How the West got China's social credit system wrong. Wired. 

This article critically examines the misconceptions and misunderstandings surrounding China's SCS in Western media. It argues that the SCS is often portrayed as a monolithic and dystopian surveillance mechanism, but in reality, it is a complex and multifaceted initiative with varying implementations across different regions and sectors in China. The author highlights the nuances of the system, including its goals of improving financial inclusion, trust, and accountability, and contrasts these with the sensationalized narratives commonly found in Western discourse. 

Read |Kobie, N. (2019, June 7). The complicated truth about China's social credit system. Wired. 

This article provides a detailed look at China's SCS, challenging the common misconception that it is a single, centralized system of control. Instead, the SCS is described as a fragmented network of regional and sector-specific initiatives, blending government programs and private efforts like Sesame Credit. The author explores how the system operates, addresses ethical and privacy concerns, and examines its broader implications for governance and societal behavior while challenging oversimplified depictions often found in media coverage. 

Read |Wang, X. (2019, December 17). Hundreds of Chinese citizens told me what they thought about the controversial social credit system. The Conversation. 

This article from The Conversation presents findings from a 16-month ethnographic study conducted in Shanghai between 2018 and 2019. The research reveals that, contrary to widespread negative perceptions in Western media, many ordinary Chinese citizens view the SCS favorably. They see it as a tool to address prevalent issues like fraud and to promote trustworthiness within society. The study highlights a significant divergence between external critiques and internal acceptance, emphasizing the importance of understanding local perspectives when evaluating such systems. 

Read |Brussee, V. (2021, September 15). China’s social credit system is actually quite boring. Foreign Policy.  

This article examines misconceptions about China's SCS. Contrary to exaggerated portrayals of it as an all-encompassing surveillance tool, the author argues that the SCS primarily targets businesses rather than individuals. Designed to improve corporate compliance and transparency, it focuses on regulatory adherence and financial credibility. The article highlights that the SCS is a decentralized framework with diverse implementations across regions and sectors. It also suggests that the system's complexity and bureaucratic nature make it far less authoritarian than often assumed, underscoring the need to understand its practical functions and limitations.

Read |Barney, N., & Wigmore, I. (n.d.). What is surveillance capitalism? TechTarget.

This article examines the concept of surveillance capitalism, a term used to describe the monetization of personal data obtained by monitoring individuals' online and offline behaviors. It explains how the term originated and emphasizes that the goal of collecting personal data—such as search histories, social media activity, and physical locations—is to create predictive products. These products are then sold to businesses to influence consumer behavior through targeted advertising and marketing strategies. The article also highlights the practices of major technology companies like Amazon, Apple, Google, and Facebook, raising concerns about privacy and the ethical implications of large-scale data collection. 

Consider these questions after reading this article: 

How does surveillance capitalism differ from traditional forms of capitalism in terms of data usage and monetization? 
What ethical concerns arise from the collection and use of personal data in surveillance capitalism? 
How might surveillance capitalism impact societal trust in technology companies? 
Would you feel comfortable sharing your personal data with companies if you knew it would improve their services? Why or why not? 
Read |United States House Committee on Oversight and Accountability. (2023, February 8). The cover-up: Big tech, the swamp, and mainstream media coordinated to censor Americans’ free speech.

This article, published by the United States House Committee on Oversight and Accountability, discusses a hearing held on February 8, 2023. The hearing examined how Twitter (now known as X), under the leadership of former executives Vijaya Gadde, James Baker, and Yoel Roth, allegedly collaborated with the FBI and federal government to monitor and censor Americans' online speech, particularly targeting conservative voices and news that contradicted mainstream narratives. The article highlights concerns about social media platforms' influence on public opinion and behavior, emphasizing the need to protect free speech from government interference and social media bias. 

Consider these questions after reading this article: 

What role does data play in shaping modern business practices, as illustrated by the article? 
How does the use of data-driven technologies influence democratic processes and free speech? 
As a future data scientist, how would you approach situations where data is being used in potentially harmful or misleading ways? 
Resource | Totenberg, N., & Thomas, J. (2024, June 26). Supreme Court rules in favor of Biden administration in social media case. NPR.

This resource includes an NPR audio segment and an article about the U.S. Supreme Court decision supporting the administration, allowing government communication with social media companies about content moderation. Six of the nine justices found no proof that the government forced the platforms to act and highlighted the importance of accuracy. They decided that the complainants could not bring the case because the platforms made their decisions on their own. Experts call for clearer rules on government influence over online content, with the decision setting stricter requirements for future lawsuits. 

Consider these questions after reviewing this resource: 

Social media platforms often use algorithms to moderate content. How might these algorithms impact the information you see as a user?  
Based on this case, what are the potential ethical or professional challenges you might face in your future career if you are involved in designing or managing such systems?
