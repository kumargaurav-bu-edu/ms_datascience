The Agentic AI MOOC gave me a broad view of what it actually takes to build and run AI agents in the real world. I joined the course to understand the agentic AI ecosystem better and to figure out what I should really be focusing on when working with LLM-based systems, beyond demos and quick prototypes.

I am a Staff IT Data Engineer working with the Data Analytics and AI team. Most of my work revolves around AI-driven tools for data governance and analytics, and more recently, I have been building agent-based use cases. So a lot that the course offered felt directly relevant to the problems I see at work.

The first lecture, which is “LLM Agents Overview”, to be honest, I did not fully understand it the first time I watched it. It felt dense, and a lot of details went over my head. Still, I get a sense of how big the AI space really is and where some of the important pieces fit. I had to watch it twice to really understand the key concepts and the terms, and the lecture is helpful in establishing the foundation before moving to the harder stuff. 

The course talked about approaches like supervised fine-tuning, reinforcement learning, and RLHF. This changed my thought process about LLM Agents. There are multiple ways you can optimize and fine-tune the LLM and Agents beyond just prompt engineering itself to get the desired results. Earlier on, I used to think that if you had a decent LLM model, you just need to write a good prompt to obtain the desired results, as AI is intelligent enough to understand the context and provide you with the output. However, this is not the case in real-life scenarios and my work, and this course helped me understand why. To get the consistent and reliable results, you need to understand how models are trained, tuned, and evaluated – and which techniques make sense for which problems. I plan to explore fine-tuning technique and structured evaluation in my current agent use case to improve reliability.

The lectures on the agent evaluation were very helpful. Coming from a software background, the analogy to traditional software testing made sense. Similar to any traditional software system, where testing is required before releasing it to users, evaluation plays a similar role for AI agents. AI agents also need structured evaluation and testing before being used by the end users. It helps you understand how an agent behaves across different scenarios. It also helps you compare different models or agent setups. I did not fully understand the details of all the benchmarks right away, but learning about the utilities and tools like CyberGym, T-Bench, GDPval, and CRMArena, I realized that people are actively doing this at scale.

One practical idea that has stuck with me was using a “Green agent” to test other agents. This was explained in the lecture “Agent Evaluation & Project Overview” on how this can be done step by step. Although I am still learning the details but it has given me a better way of thinking about testing agent behavior instead of relying only on manual checks and instincts. For example, I might implement a small-scale automated evaluation framework for our internal agents, using a green agent to test other agents and using benchmarks like T-Bench to measure the performance systematically.

The guest lectures made the course feel realistic. For example, Weizhu Chen, Microsoft, in the lecture “Some Challenges and Lessons from Training Agentic Models” spoke about how a large part of the effort in training the agentic model goes into just making training work, which helped set more realistic expectations. He also spoke about the long-context issues, something I now realize is a common problem in many agentic setups, and how techniques like summarization are used in practice. On the other hand, Noam Brown’s in his lecture on multi-agent AI spoke about more advanced topics like AlphaGo Analogy, Recursive self-improvement (self-play), and minmax equilibrium (a set of strategies in which no player can improve by deviating). He also spoke about the difficulties and shortcomings of the multi-agent system in achieving parallelism and efficiency when communicating with humans. This was a little tough for me because I don’t know much about game theory, but still informative and helped me see how complex multi-agent systems work.

I also completely agree with the idea shared by Clay Bavor, Sierra in the lecture “Practical Lessons from Deploying Real-World AI agents” that building an agent is just the tip of the iceberg. Once you do deeper, optimization, evaluation, and reliability become much harder problems than we initially think. Security was a topic that was brought up several times during the course, and one that I think is one of the most critical aspects of building agentic AI systems. Unlike traditional software, AI agents inherit all the usual security risks that a traditional software system has and add new, less predictable ones. In agent-based systems, there are multiple attack vectors to think about, including direct prompt injection, heuristic and optimization-based attacks, agent poisoning, and misuse of tool access. This made it clear that securing an AI system cannot be treated as a one-time check or a single control. It requires a layered approach, which combines traditional security practices with new safeguards specific to non-deterministic and autonomous behavior. Compared to conventional applications, the security surface for AI agents is significantly larger, and ignoring this complexity can easily undermine an otherwise well-designed system.

Conclusion
Overall, this course gave me a much clearer picture of the agentic AI ecosystem. There were guest lectures, overviews of LLM agents, training methods, benchmarking, and the really important topics of security and safety. I had been wondering for a long time if there was a course that could walk me through some of the important aspects of agentic AI and give me an idea of what to focus on. This course isn’t about teaching you how to build an LLM Agent from scratch, but it does give you an idea of all the moving pieces, the challenges that come up in real-world systems, and examples of how teams actually solve them. For me, it helped connect a lot of dots and gave me a better sense of what really matters when building AI agents that work reliably in practice.

Key takeaways from this course
Building AI agents is only the tip of the iceberg; infrastructure, evaluation, and security are equally critical.
Fine-tuning, RLHF, and structured evaluation are important for consistent results.
Testing and benchmarking tools (e.g. T-Bench, GCPval) can be used to make it more reliable.
Multi-agent systems are complex and require careful monitoring.
Security for AI agents is multi-layered and ongoing, not a one-time process.
